I run an agent workforce. It has a charter, an approval gate, and evals.

Most people show you an AI demo. Mine runs my work every day, and you can inspect it.

It covers a small team's worth of work while I am at my day job: triage, research, drafting, and checking its own output. I trust it because I can check it. Every action leaves an audit trail, every procedure has tests, and every decision is written down.

### The workspace: [plim](https://github.com/zankkas/plim)

An agent with no rules invents its own habits every session. It reconstructs status from memory, duplicates trackers, forgets decisions, and applies its own judgment where authority was already defined.

`plim` is the kit that stops that. Not designed in the abstract. It is extracted from the workspace that actually runs mine. Forty-three procedures with eval cases. A doctor that verifies state before any claim is made. Decisions written down the moment they land. Anything irreversible waits for a human.

It runs daily. When it drifts, it says so.

### The machine: [spx-omarchy](https://github.com/zankkas/spx-omarchy)

A Surface Pro X ships with 8 GB of RAM and spends 6 to 7 of it on Windows before you open anything.

Mine now runs Omarchy: an Arch and Hyprland desktop, on ARM, installed by script and in daily service. Keyboard first, tiling by default, nothing fighting the terminal.

Getting there took an installer that stops rather than guesses, and forty-six regression tests. And an agent skill that takes you from zero to a complete Omarchy setup on a Surface Pro X. You hand it to your agent, it does most of the work. What is left is a USB stick, the device itself, and one or two commands.

This is the part of the job nobody puts on a slide.

## How I work

**Think big, execute small.** Big plans set the direction, small steps get there. On a bad day, discipline is gone and only the structure I built is still standing. So I plan for my worst self. Anything I have to remember is a liability, so every mistake I make twice becomes a check the system runs instead. That habit became `plim`.

**Treasure and dragons share an address.** Whatever is worth having usually sits behind something inconvenient. Behind that fight sat a thin Linux setup that makes AI work fast and remote, on a good machine Windows had made hard to use. Knowing what was behind the fight is what made it worth finishing.

**Only the load-bearing words.** Minimalism is not a style, it is respect for the reader. A sentence that adds nothing takes something away, which is attention. So I cut until what is left carries weight. Same rule for prompts, specs, and this page.

**A list of facts is not a story.** Facts in order only tell you what happened. Meaning comes from how one led to the next, and from where the expected thing did not happen. I write that way. I debug that way too. Both are a search for the step where the chain breaks.
